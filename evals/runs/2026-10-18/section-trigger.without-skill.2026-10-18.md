## What this means for the pitch

**1. Show take rate from both sides.**
DoorDash revenue as a % of marketplace GOV comes from merchant commissions plus consumer fees plus ads. Don’t show only commission rate.

**2. Show merchant economics, not just merchant revenue.**
A restaurant pays commission to get incremental orders. If those orders would have come through the restaurant’s own channel, DoorDash is cannibalizing margin. The pitch needs evidence of incremental demand: new customers, higher frequency, larger baskets, or off-premise volume the restaurant couldn’t reach alone.

**3. Show consumer fee sensitivity.**
Consumers pay fees, but DashPass and subsidies reduce friction. Show order frequency and retention by DashPass vs. non-DashPass. If fees rise and frequency falls, the two-sided model breaks.

**4. Show ad attach rate.**
Advertising is the cleanest margin in the model. Show ad revenue as % of revenue, merchant adoption, and whether ads are incremental or just shifting existing commission dollars.

**5. Show the flywheel — and the tension.**
More restaurants → better selection → more consumers → more orders → more merchant demand → more ad inventory. But raising merchant commissions can push restaurants to their own channels, and raising consumer fees can cut frequency. DoorDash’s pricing power is constrained by both sides.

## The claim

Order frequency rises, courier cost per order stays flat, so contribution profit per order improves and the fixed cost base gets leveraged.

For that to work, several things have to be true at once.

## What would have to be true

**1. Frequency gains come from density, not from longer trips.**
More orders per customer only help if they land in the same trade areas. If growth comes from new, thinner suburbs, each order is a longer drive and courier cost per order rises. So: order growth must be concentrated where Dasher supply already exists.

**2. Batching improves as frequency rises.**
Flat courier cost per order with rising volume means Dashers carry more than one order per trip, or take shorter deadhead miles. If batching doesn’t improve, more orders just means more Dasher hours at the same cost — no operating leverage.

**3. Dasher pay stays stable per order.**
That requires adequate Dasher supply, no regulatory reclassification, no minimum-pay mandates, and no tip inflation. Any of those raises cost per order directly. Watch state and city labor rules.

**4. Consumer fees don’t get competed away.**
If frequency rises because DoorDash cut fees or expanded DashPass discounts, revenue per order falls even if cost per order is flat. Contribution per order can still compress. Frequency growth has to come with stable or rising revenue per order.

**5. Merchant commissions hold.**
Restaurants have alternatives — Uber Eats, own channels, pickup. If commission rates erode to defend frequency, the revenue side of the equation weakens even with flat courier cost.

**6. Basket size holds or grows.**
Suburban orders tend to be larger. If frequency rises but average order value falls (smaller, more frequent orders), delivery cost per dollar of food rises. Flat cost per order with a smaller basket is worse economics, not better.

**7. Advertising scales with frequency.**
More orders means more ad impressions and more merchant demand for placement. That’s the margin kicker. If ad load is capped to protect the consumer experience, the ad benefit plateaus and you’re back to thin delivery margins.

**8. Fixed costs stay fixed.**
The leverage story assumes platform, support, and corporate costs don’t scale with orders. If support tickets, refunds, and fraud rise with volume, “flat courier cost” doesn’t mean flat total cost.

## What would break it

- New-market expansion into low-density suburbs
- Dasher pay regulation or minimum-earnings rules
- Fee competition from Uber Eats or Instacart
- Merchant commission pushback or own-channel shift
- Smaller baskets from more frequent, lower-value orders
- Ad load caps or merchant ad fatigue
- Rising refunds, fraud, or support cost per order

## Why the margin might never show up

**1. Take rates are structurally lower.**
Grocery merchants run 1–3% net margins. They cannot pay restaurant-level commissions. New Verticals take rate is materially below Marketplace. More grocery volume at a lower take rate dilutes blended revenue per order even as order count rises.

**2. Cost to serve is higher, not flat.**
Grocery orders require in-store shopping time, substitutions, out-of-stock handling, cold chain, and larger loads. Dasher minutes per order rise. That breaks the “courier cost per order stays flat” condition from your earlier slide — the two assumptions are linked.

**3. Errors are costlier.**
Substitutions, missing items, and spoiled goods drive refunds and support contacts. Refund rate per order is higher in grocery than restaurant. That hits contribution directly.

**4. The ad layer doesn’t transfer cleanly.**
Restaurant ads are local merchant demand-gen: many small advertisers, high attach, easy to sell. Grocery ads are CPG retail media: fewer, larger advertisers, a different sales motion, and DoorDash competes against Instacart’s established retail media business. Ad revenue per order in grocery is likely lower, not higher.

**5. Mix shift dilutes the blend.**
Even if absolute contribution profit grows, blended contribution margin per order can fall as grocery becomes a larger share. The market values the blend. A lower-margin mix looks like a delivery company again — which is exactly the perception you’re trying to argue against.

**6. Basket size helps less than it looks.**
A bigger grocery basket spreads delivery cost over more dollars, which looks good on cost per dollar. But the revenue take is a smaller percentage, and the error/refund cost is higher. Big basket, thin take, costly mistakes.
