## The claim, stated plainly

The market values DoorDash on delivery economics: thin contribution margin per order, heavy courier cost, competitive discounting. That’s the right lens for the delivery P&L and the wrong lens for the consolidated business, because advertising revenue carries near-100% incremental margin. Every ad dollar that lands on top of the existing order flow drops almost entirely to profit. So the correct question isn’t “what’s the delivery margin,” it’s “what share of profit comes from ads, and what multiple does that deserve.”

## Slide structure

**Box 1 — What the market sees**
- Delivery as a low-margin, labor-intensive, competitive business
- Valued on delivery contribution margin and order growth
- Ad revenue treated as a small, bundled, hard-to-see line item

**Box 2 — What’s actually true**
- Ads ride on demand that already exists — no incremental courier cost, no incremental consumer acquisition
- Ad revenue grows with merchant competition for visibility, not with order volume alone
- Incremental ad dollars convert to profit at a much higher rate than incremental delivery dollars

**Box 3 — Why the gap exists**
- Ads are reported inside a bundled “advertising and other” line, so the profit mix isn’t visible on the face of the P&L
- Sell-side models tend to forecast ads as a % of GOV and stop there, rather than modeling the margin mix
- Result: the market applies a delivery multiple to an ad-weighted profit stream

## The one chart that carries the slide

A stacked bar of **profit mix**: delivery contribution vs. advertising contribution, today vs. your forecast year. If ads go from, say, a fifth of profit to a third, that single visual does more work than three paragraphs. Pair it with a second small chart: ad revenue as % of GOV over time.

## The valuation bridge

This is where the mispricing becomes a price target. Two options:

1. **Sum-of-the-parts** — value the delivery business on a delivery multiple, value the ad business on an ad or marketplace multiple, add them. Show the blended multiple the market is implicitly paying vs. the blended multiple your SOTP implies.

## First, define it precisely

“Courier cost per order stays flat” can mean three different things, and a PM will ask which one you mean:

- **Nominal dollars per order** flat
- **Cost per order as % of GOV** flat (i.e., flat take on a rising ticket)
- **Cost per order flat while courier pay per delivery rises** (only possible if batching improves)

Pick one and state it. I’d use nominal dollars per order, because that’s the version that protects contribution margin.

## The mechanism you’re implicitly betting on

Frequency → density → batching → cost per order down. But frequency also pushes into peak saturation → surge pay → cost per order up. Your assumption says those two forces cancel. That’s a real bet, and it needs four sub-conditions.

## What would have to be true

**1. Batching rises.** More orders per courier trip is the main lever that offsets higher pay per delivery. Requires orders to cluster in the same restaurant and same neighborhood at the same time. Test: orders per delivery trip. Tripwire: batching flattens for two quarters.

**2. Frequency growth comes from existing dense suburbs, not new thin ones.** Adding orders in a suburb you already serve lowers cost per order. Expanding into a thinner adjacent suburb raises it. Your assumption only works if growth is infill, not expansion. Test: order growth split between existing and new zones. Tripwire: new-zone orders become a rising share of growth.

**3. Courier supply keeps pace at dinner peak.** If frequency rises faster than courier hours, you get unassigned orders, re-dispatch, and surge incentives — all of which show up as cost per order. Test: unassigned order rate, peak completion rate, incentive spend per order. Tripwire: peak completion rate falls.

**4. Restaurant prep time doesn’t worsen.** Courier wait at the restaurant is a direct cost — idle time or a re-dispatched order. More orders per restaurant at peak can lengthen prep queues. Test: courier wait time per order. Tripwire: wait time rises with frequency.

## The two things that break it regardless of frequency

- **Regulation.** Reclassification or minimum-pay rules reset courier cost per order upward independent of density. This is a step change, not a slope.
- **Distance and ticket mix.** If frequency growth skews to longer-distance or lower-ticket orders, cost per order rises even with perfect batching.

## The useful pitch point

You’re assuming **flat**. Density logic says cost per order should *decline* as frequency rises in existing suburbs. So if history shows a mild decline, your flat assumption already has cushion built in — say that explicitly. It turns a defensive assumption into an embedded margin of safety.

Frame it as: “We assume courier cost per order is flat. Density math suggests it should fall. We’re not giving ourselves credit for the decline, which is where the upside to our target comes from.”

## Why this specific risk is the one that kills the thesis

Your whole argument is a profit-mix argument: ads carry near-100% incremental margin, so a rising ad share of profit justifies a re-rating. Grocery and retail break that in three places at once:

- **Commission is structurally lower.** Restaurant take rates run in the mid-to-high teens. Grocery commissions are single-digit. Same GOV dollar, far less revenue.
- **Ad attach per GOV dollar is lower.** Restaurant merchants are fragmented local businesses competing for visibility in a small radius — that's a great ad market. Grocery is a handful of consolidated chains with their own retail media networks and real negotiating leverage. They will not pay restaurant-level ad rates.
- **Cost per order is higher.** Heavier baskets, more items, substitutions, refunds, cold-chain handling, and more courier time per delivery.

So grocery volume grows GOV and revenue while pushing the profit mix *back toward* delivery and *away from* ads. That's the exact opposite of your thesis. If it happens, the market's "low-margin delivery company" framing turns out to be correct, and your multiple never shows up.

## What would confirm you're wrong

Set thresholds now, before the data arrives, so you can't rationalize later:

| Signal | Tripwire |
|---|---|
| Grocery/retail share of GOV | Rising for 3+ consecutive quarters |
| Blended take rate | Declining as grocery mix rises |
| Ad revenue as % of GOV | Flat or falling despite GOV growth |

| Ad revenue growth vs. restaurant order growth | Decoupling — ads no longer outgrowing restaurant volume |

The cleanest single test: **does ad revenue keep growing faster than total GOV?** If yes, the ad layer is scaling with the business and the thesis holds. If ad revenue growth converges toward GOV growth, the ad layer is being diluted by low-monetization volume — that's your thesis dying in slow motion.

## The distinction that matters

Two versions of grocery growth, and only one is bad:

- **Good version:** grocery GOV grows and ad revenue grows with it, because grocery merchants buy ads at a decent attach rate. Thesis intact, TAM larger.
- **Bad version:** grocery GOV grows and ad attach per grocery dollar is structurally much lower than restaurant. GOV up, profit mix dilutes, multiple never expands.

Your slide should say you expect the first and name the second as the falsifier. Don't let the audience collapse them.
