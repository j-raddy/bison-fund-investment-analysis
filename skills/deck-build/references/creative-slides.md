# Creative and Optional Slides

A menu of slides beyond the five required sections. Students pick two or three that fit their
company and their story. These are where pitches stop looking templated and start looking like
they belong to the analyst who wrote them. Everything here follows the same rules as the core
deck: every figure sourced and dated, no acronyms, plain titles, Key takeaway boxes, charts
instead of paragraphs.

## Vision and story

1. **The Big Vision.** One slide, one sentence: what does this company look like in ten years if
   everything the thesis believes comes true? Write it in the analyst's own words, no hedging.
   This is the emotional open of the pitch, and the rest of the deck has to earn it.
2. **Founder story.** Who built this and why they are the right person to run it. Track record,
   capital allocation history, and how they think. Skip marketing; the record is the story.
3. **What the world looks like in 2035.** The demand-side version of the thesis: what changes in
   the customer's life, and why this company sits in the middle of it.

## Market and business model

4. **TAM, built not borrowed.** The market size computed from the bottom up (customers times
   spend), not quoted from a research note. Show the build; a quoted TAM is worth less than a
   constructed one.
5. **Unit economics deep dive.** One customer's economics end to end: acquisition cost, revenue,
   contribution margin, payback period, lifetime value. The best pitches can draw this from memory.
6. **Pricing power.** Evidence the company can raise prices: price increases passed through,
   take-rate changes, mix shift toward premium.

## Analysis and debate

7. **The bear debate.** Two columns: the strongest short case in the analyst's own words, and
   the rebuttal with evidence. Presenting the bear case as a live debate teaches more than any
   bull slide.
8. **Moat stress test.** Take the moat claim and attack it directly: who is attacking it, what
   happens if a specific competitor or technology succeeds, and what the evidence says.
9. **Mental models.** The frameworks that actually drive the decision: network effects,
   switching costs, scale economies, or a second-hand car market logic. State which model fits
   and which does not.
10. **Scenarios, not forecasts.** Three dated worlds (recession, status quo, acceleration) with
    what the company earns in each. Better than a single point estimate for a business with
    cyclical demand.

## Evidence and craft

11. **Precedent transactions.** What the market has paid for similar businesses, and what the
    current price implies versus those deals.
12. **Ownership and insider activity.** Who holds the stock, what insiders have bought or sold,
    and what the compensation structure pays them to do.
13. **The five questions.** End with the five questions the analyst would ask if they were the
    skeptic in the room, each with the one-line answer and the source. Doubles as question prep.
14. **A contrarian data point.** One number most people covering the stock have not looked at,
    with the source and why it changes the picture. The signature slide of a good student pitch.

## How to use the menu

- Two or three additions are enough. The core five sections carry the pitch; additions earn their
  place by changing how the audience thinks.
- Each optional slide needs the same evidence standard as the core deck.
- If an optional slide cannot be sourced, it does not go in the deck; it goes in the notes and
  becomes a question for the room.
